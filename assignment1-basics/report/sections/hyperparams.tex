\section{Experiments}

\problem{experiment\_log}{Experiment logging}{3}

Each run records configuration, losses, LR, steps/tokens, elapsed time, and
checkpoints in \texttt{train.log} and W\&B. The low-resource TinyStories
experiments use MPS and about 40.96M tokens; the added TinyStories/OWT main
runs use CUDA and 327.68M tokens. Logging details, shared settings, and
additional diagnostics are in Appendices~\ref{app:experiment_details}--\ref{app:diagnostics}.

\problem{learning\_rate}{Tune the learning rate}{3}

\textbf{(a) Low-resource search.}
I screened three LRs for 1,000 steps, then compared peaks
$5\times10^{-4}$, $10^{-3}$, and $2\times10^{-3}$ in fresh 10,000-step
runs (40.96M tokens each). All use the same four-layer TinyStories model,
batch 16, seed 42, and cosine floor equal to one tenth of the peak.
The lowest final loss, 1.5966, came from $10^{-3}$ and met the 2.00
low-resource target. I retained this LR for subsequent baselines.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
\textbf{(b) Stability.}
Larger tested LRs first slowed learning, then caused repeated loss
explosions at 0.3/0.5. Losses stayed finite and sometimes recovered; this
is instability, not proven unbounded divergence. These short probes used
the 10,000-step schedule and are compared over their first 1,000 updates.
The best tested LR was well below the unstable range, so the search does
not establish a sharp ``edge of stability.''

\textbf{(c) Standard-budget TinyStories follow-up.}
\phantomsection\label{par:standard_ts}
A fresh baseline retained LR $10^{-3}/10^{-4}$ and trained for 80,000
updates (327.68M tokens), with 800-step warmup and the same fixed validation
windows as the matched low-resource baseline. Final loss was 1.3245,
meeting the standard target of 1.45; the sampled minimum was 1.3240 at
79,200 updates. Complete settings are in Appendix~\ref{app:standard_protocol}.

The matched short run ended at 1.5879, a gap of 0.2634 nats per token.
Its earlier advantage at equal token counts reverses as the long run

The low-resource ablations were not repeated at the larger budget.

\problem{batch\_size\_experiment}{Batch size variations}{1}

I varied batch size while keeping approximately 40.96M training tokens and
LR $10^{-3}/10^{-4}$ fixed. Updates, warmup, and evaluation/saving intervals
scaled inversely with batch size; all runs used the same private validation
windows (seed 42, 20 batches of 16 sequences).

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \small
    \begin{tabular}{@{}rrrrrr@{}}
        \toprule
        Batch & Updates & Warmup & Eval interval & Final val loss & Time (min) \\
        \midrule
        1  & 160,000 & 1,600 & 1,600 & 1.7321 & 144.34 \\
        8  & 20,000  & 200   & 200   & \textbf{1.5839} & 89.51 \\
        16 & 10,000  & 100   & 100   & 1.5879 & 87.03 \\
        32 & 5,000   & 50    & 50    & 1.6081 & 85.51 \\
        48 & 3,333   & 33    & 33    & 1.6329 & 84.62 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Approximately equal-token, fixed-LR batch-size comparison on MPS.
    Warmup and evaluation intervals are in updates. Times use the last logged
    training elapsed time and include intervening evaluation/checkpoint work,
    but exclude startup and final evaluation/checkpoint overhead.}
    \label{tab:batch_size_results}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/batchsize_train.png}\hfill
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/batchsize_val.png}
    \caption{W\&B training loss (left) and validation loss (right) against
    tokens processed, with smoothing disabled. Legend numbers denote batch
    sizes; colors match across panels: magenta 1, gray 8, orange 16, red 32,
    and brown 48. Training points are individual minibatch losses.}
    \label{fig:batch_size}
\end{figure}

Batches 8--16 gave the best observed loss/runtime balance. Batch 8's 1.5839
was only 0.0040 below batch 16, insufficient for a reliable ranking from
one seed. Batch 1 fluctuated more and learned less efficiently; batches
32/48 were slightly faster but had worse final losses. Increasing 16 to
48 saved only about 2.8\% elapsed time.

These are fixed-LR recipes, not per-batch optima. Batch 1's system-wide
swap growth confounds timing. Capacity probes passed at 48 but stopped at
56/64 for memory pressure, so 48 is the largest tested passing batch,
not a proven limit. Diagnostics and run-selection details are in
Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics}.

\problem{generate}{Generate text}{1}
\label{prob:generate}

\textbf{Low-resource samples.}
The matched TinyStories baseline (loss 1.5879) used the Lily prompt,
seed 42, top-$p=0.9$, and temperatures 0.7/1.0/1.3, with a maximum of
256 new tokens. All stopped at EOS. Full outputs are in
Appendix~\ref{app:low_resource_samples}.

        \midrule
        0.7 & 13 & 142 & 141 & EOS \\
        1.0 & 13 & 207 & 206 & EOS \\
        1.3 & 13 & 157 & 156 & EOS \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Counts reported by the generation CLI. Sampled tokens include

Lower temperatures produced readable story structure but inconsistent
logic: the mother returns the ball twice at 0.7, while 1.0 shifts from a
rose to an apple and a flower. At 1.3, phrases such as ``grandma mommy''
and ``cupboardving'' weaken fluency.

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\textbf{Standard-budget samples.}
Both final models use the same three prompts:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{P\arabic*:},leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item \texttt{Once upon a time, there was a little girl named Lily.}
\item \texttt{In a recent study, researchers found that}
\item \texttt{The history of the Internet began with}
\end{enumerate}
Each prompt is sampled at 0.7/1.0/1.3 with seed 42 reset, top-$p=0.9$,
CUDA, and a 256-new-token/EOS limit. Appendix~\ref{app:standard_samples}
contains all 18 outputs and token counts. The excerpts below use 0.7.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
\textbf{P1: story.}
\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{TinyStories:} She had a big box of crayons. [\ldots]
"What a nice picture!" she said.

\textbf{OWT:} When she got the chance to pick up the rope, she became aware
of what was going on, and was seen in a scene in a police car.
\end{quote}
TinyStories sustains a story but confuses drawing with cake-making. OWT
switches to repetitive incident reporting. P1 favors TinyStories' domain.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
\textbf{P2: research.}
\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{TinyStories:} The people in the village were worried about their customers.

\textbf{OWT:} The researchers are also examining the effects of lead-freezing
in brain cells, which may cause lead-freezing.
\end{quote}
TinyStories returns to stories; OWT imitates scientific style but produces
circular, unsupported explanations. Style matching is not factual coherence.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
\textbf{P3: Internet history.}
\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{TinyStories:} The history of the Internet began with a smile.

\textbf{OWT:} The history of the Internet began with the introduction of the
new Internet-based Internet.
\end{quote}
TinyStories again returns to its story distribution. OWT stays on technology
but repeats phrases rather than producing a coherent history.

Both longer runs improved held-out loss without eliminating contradictions.
Temperature 1.3 increased malformed words and topic drift; lower temperatures
could favor repetition. These one-seed samples do not establish an optimal
temperature or a general quality ranking. Low-resource OWT used only P1,
so P2/P3 do not test generation changes across matched budgets.
